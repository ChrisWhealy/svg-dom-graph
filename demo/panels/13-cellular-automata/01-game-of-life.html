Conway's Game of Life

The Game of Life was invented by the mathematician John Conway in 1970. It is a very unusual game in that it is designed for zero players. You set up a starting pattern of live cells on a grid, then watch what happens.

Every cell has eight neighbours: the four cells above, below, left and right of it, and the four diagonals. All the cells are updated at once from the current generation to the next according to some very simple rules.

Does the live cell survive to the next generation? Well, that depends on the number of neighbours it currently has:

  • Zero or one, then it dies of loneliness
  • Two or three, then it stays alive.
  • More than three, then it dies of overcrowding.

A dead cell with exactly three live neighbours comes back to life.

From these rules come patterns that sit still, patterns that repeat, patterns that travel across the board, and patterns that grow for hundreds of generations before they settle down.

Using the board

  • Click a cell to bring it to life or kill it. Drag across the board to give every cell you pass over the same state as the first.
  • Tab to the board, then use the arrow keys to move the red cursor, and the Space or Enter key to change the cell under it.
  • Play or Pause starts and stops the generations, and Step works out just one. The slider sets how many there are in each second.
  • Random fills the board with scattered cells, and Clear empties it.
  • Wrap edges decides what happens at the edge of the board. When it is on, the left edge is next to the right edge and the top is next to the bottom, so a pattern that leaves one side comes back in at the other. When it is off, everything beyond the edge is dead.
  • The first row of buttons under the board replaces what is on it with a pattern. The second row adds a spaceship to what is already there, at the left edge and level with the red cursor, and it flies to the right. Move the cursor first with the arrow keys to choose the height.

Some patterns to try

The buttons under the board replace what is on it with one of these.

Glider
Five cells that crawl diagonally across the board, one cell every four generations. With the edges wrapped it goes round for ever. Without, it ends as a still block when it hits the corner.
Blinker
Three cells in a row, which turn into a column and back again. It is the simplest oscillator.
R-pentomino
Just five cells, but it does not settle down for 1103 generations on an unlimited board, and sends out gliders on the way. On this small board with the edges wrapped, the gliders soon meet it again, so what you see is different.
Acorn
Seven cells that take 5206 generations to settle down on an unlimited board.
Glider gun
Bill Gosper's gun, discovered in 1970, which sends out a new glider every 30 generations. It was the first pattern found that grows without limit. Turn off Wrap edges to see the gliders leave the board without running into the gun again.

Spaceships

A spaceship is a pattern that repeats itself every few generations in a different place, so it flies across the board. The glider is the smallest. Conway also found three that fly along a row, which differ in size:

Light spaceship
Nine cells.
Medium spaceship
Eleven cells.
Heavy spaceship
Thirteen cells.

Each one repeats every four generations, two cells to the right, so it flies at half a cell a generation. Where the edges wrap, it goes round the board and comes back. Where they stop, it runs into the edge and is broken up. Add one from the second row of buttons while a glider or the gun is going, and watch what happens when they meet.

Why it matters

The Game of Life is a cellular automaton: a grid of cells, each with a state, that all change together by a simple rule that only looks at the neighbours. Despite this, it is Turing complete. With enough cells, a pattern can be built that does any calculation that a computer can do.
