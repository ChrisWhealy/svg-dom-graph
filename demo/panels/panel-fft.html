Fast Fourier Transform

What problem does it solve?

When a signal such as a sound wave, a radio wave or vibration in a building is recorded, what you end up with is a list of amplitude measurements taken at regular (usually very small) time intervals. Whilst this list shows how the signal changes over time, it hides the individual frequencies from which that signal is composed. Consider a chord played on the piano. The waveform captured by the microphone is a complex signal, with very little visible structure; yet, that chord is composed of only three or four individual notes.

The Fourier transform answers the question “What are the individual frequencies that make up this signal?” It takes N measurements and gives back N numbers, one for each frequency. Each number says how strong that particular frequency is and where in its cycle it starts. That second part is why an FFT works with complex numbers: a complex number encodes both a magnitude and a direction.

Worked out straight from its definition, every one of the N answers needs a sum over all N measurements, so the amount of work grows in proportion to N × N. One second of CD-quality audio contains 44,100 individual measurements which, when squared, would require us to perform about 1.94 billion multiplications!

However, the fast in Fast Fourier transform (FFT) allows us to reduce the number of multiplications down to N × log2 N. So for one second of CD-quality audio signal, the number of calculations drops from 1.94 billion to around 700,000. That speed increase now makes realtime analysis a practical reality.

Try it: find the waves inside a sum

Three sine waves are drawn below. Wave A completes one cycle in the window, wave B two, and wave C three. Each has an amplitude, which is how tall it is, and a phase, which is how far along its cycle it is at the left edge. The thick line is the three added together. Where the waves rise together they reinforce one another, and where one rises as another falls they cancel.

The orange dots are the samples: the sum measured at evenly spaced moments. The FFT sees only those dots. It is never told that the signal is made of three waves, how tall they are or where they start. The transform gives one complex number for each frequency. Its length divided by half the number of samples is the amplitude, and its angle, turned a quarter of a cycle because these are sine waves and not cosines, is the phase. A frequency that none of the waves uses comes out as zero. Eight samples can only tell apart frequencies below four cycles, so all three waves are within reach.

How is it fast?

The saving comes from one observation: the transform of N measurements can be built from two transforms of N/2 measurements, one of the even-numbered measurements and one of the odd-numbered, joined by a small calculation. Split again and again until each piece is a single measurement, and there is nothing left to transform. All of the work is in the joining.

The "Radix-2" part of the name means that every measurement is split in two, and the "decimation in time" part means that the split is made on the measurements in time order.

The small calculation is the butterfly operation. It reads two numbers a and b and a twiddle factor w.

b is multipled by the twiddle factor to give a + w·b. This has the effect of rotating b in the complex plane by some fixed part of a turn.

The Butterfly operation then returns the sum a + w·b and the difference a − w·b.

Watching the FFT work

The diagram below is the transform of the samples in the graph. It is drawn again when you let go of a slider.

  • The first column, S0, holds the measurements in bit-reversed order. This is the order that repeated even and odd splitting leaves them in, and it puts each pair that must be joined side by side.
  • Each press of Next completes one stage. A stage joins blocks that are twice as wide as the stage before, so the transform is finished after log2 N stages. A faint zero is a number that has not been worked out yet.
  • The last column holds the transform X[0], X[1] and so on, already in order.
  • The four connectors between two columns are one butterfly. The butterfly you choose from the list is emphasised, and the second scene below opens it up: its two inputs, its twiddle factor, and the product, sum and difference. You can drag the frame.
  • Set two of the waves to zero. The last column then has just two nonzero results for the remaining wave, X[k] and X[N−k], which are mirror images of one another. That is why the table reads only the lower of the two.

The numbers are the forward transform, X[k] = the sum of x[n] × e−2πikn/N over all n, with no scaling. As everywhere else in this gallery, the library does no arithmetic. Every number is worked out in plain Rust, one whole block at a time, and the nodes only show it.
