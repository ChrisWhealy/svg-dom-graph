Hamming(7,4)

A Hamming code adds a few extra bits to some data so that a receiver can not only tell that a bit changed on the way, but find which bit it was, and flip it back. No other bit needs to be sent again.

Hamming(7,4) protects four data bits with three parity bits, making a codeword of seven. The bits are numbered 1 to 7 from the left. The parity bits are at the positions that are powers of two, 1, 2 and 4, and the data bits are at 3, 5, 6 and 7. Each parity bit makes a group of bits hold an even number of 1s. The group is every position whose number has the parity bit's own number among its binary 1s:

  • p1 at position 1 (binary 001) covers positions 1, 3, 5 and 7.
  • p2 at position 2 (binary 010) covers positions 2, 3, 6 and 7.
  • p4 at position 4 (binary 100) covers positions 4, 5, 6 and 7.

The receiver works out the same three groups over the bits that arrived. If a bit flipped, the groups that contain it no longer hold an even number of 1s. Written as a binary number, the three checks are the syndrome, and it is exactly the position of the flipped bit, because each position is covered by the groups that are the 1s in its number. A syndrome of zero means that every group is still even.

The toolbar at the top steps along the path. At the start only the data is shown and the unreached boxes are drawn faint. The buttons along the top are used to:

  1. Set each data bit
  2. Flip a random bit to simulate transmission corruption

The walk will start again when you:

  1. Press "Restart" (which also removes any flipped bit)
  2. Press "Previous" when the walk is already at the start

Hamming(7,4) corrects only one flipped bit. If two bits flipped, the syndrome would point at a third position, and the receiver would put the wrong bit right without knowing. That is why this demo flips only one.
