The (Non-)Pythagorean Theorem

Although no writings of Pythagoras have ever been discovered, other ancient writers such as Plato and Herodotus wrote extensively about his work.

By the period of the Roman Republic, Pythagorus was held in such high regard as a philosopher and mathematician, that it was felt appropriate to attribute this relationship to him — in spite of the fact that it had been widely known for at least 1000 years before his birth.

One of the earliest documents known to record this relationship is the Berlin Papyrus 6619 written in Ancient Egypt during the 13th Dynasty (1803 - 1649 BC).

Sohcahtoa — Sine, Cosine & Tangent

As the radius (of length c) swings around the circle, you can see that the horizontal and vertical lines (labelled b and c) form a right-angled triangle. In this diagrams, we have labelled the angle between the radius and the X axis θ, and relative to this angle, the vertical and horizontal sides of the triangle are labelled opposite and adjacent respectively.

The longest side of a right-angled triangle is always known as the hypotenuse.

In a right-angled triangle, the size of the angle θ is directly related to the ratio of the lengths of the other two sides. If you know one of these ratios, then you also know the angle. Similarly, if you know the angle, then you can calculate any one of these ratios.

By remembering the acronym SOHCAHTOA, you can remember how each of the trigonometric ratios are calculated.

Sine — SOH

The length of side opposite the angle θ divided by the length of the hypotenuse gives a ratio called Sine.

Hence SOH: Sine = Opposite over Hypotenuse

Cosine — CAH

The length of side adjacent to the angle θ divided by the length of the hypotenuse, gives a ratio called Cosine.

Hence CAH: Cosine = Adjacent over Hypotenuse

Tangent — TOA

The length of side opposite the angle θ divided by the length of the adjacent to it gives a ratio called Tangent.

Hence TOA: Tangent = Opposite over Adjacent
