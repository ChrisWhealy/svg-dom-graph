Convolution: Finding a Frequency in a Signal

Suppose we have a wave that is composed of signals whose frequencies we would like to discover. How could we dicover this?

The wave show in black is a signal composed of only two sine waves added together: one of 2 Hz and one of 5 Hz.

But to start with, we do not know this, so let's say we want to discover whether the signal contains a sine wave at a frequency of 3 Hz. To do this, we set the probe frequency to 3 Hz, then sweep a cosine and a sine wave at the probe frequency over the signal.

"Sweeping" a probe freqeuency over the signal means that for all possible phase angles, we will multiply the signal by both probe waves, then add up the result over a whole second. You can think of this as calculating a "score" for how well the probe frequency matches the signal. This is done by calculating the area between the curve and the X axis. In mathematical term, this is called taking the integral of the curve between two arbitrary positions.

There is a slight catch however. The true "score" for a particular probe frequency is calculated by treating the area above the X axis as positive (shaded blue), and the area below the X axis (shaded orange) as negative. So it quite possible that the score might add up to zero — and this is exactly what we want if there is no component in the signal at the probe frequency.

  • If the signal contains the probe frequency, the integral (or "score") will be large.
  • If the signal does not contain the probe freqency, the integral (or "score") will either be close to zero.

Drag the slider, or Tab to it and use the arrow keys, to change the frequency of the probe.

The probe frequency starts at 3 Hz. Note that the two integrals give a score of zero.

Try sliding the probe frequency around and see how the integral rises and falls it either does, or does not match a freqency present in the signal.

Why both a cosine and a sine?

A single probe is not enough. A wave of the same frequency as the probe, but a quarter of a cycle behind it, would be exactly as far above the axis as below, and the integral would be zero even though the frequency is present. The cosine and the sine are a quarter of a cycle apart, so whatever the phase of the wave in the signal, at least one of the two integrals will find it.

For a wave A · sin(2π · f · t + φ) in the signal, and a probe of frequency f, twice the two integrals are:

a = 2 ∫ s(t) · cos(2π f t) dt = A · sin φ
b = 2 ∫ s(t) · sin(2π f t) dt = A · cos φ

So the amplitude of that wave is √(a2 + b2), and its phase is the angle whose sine is proportional to a and whose cosine is proportional to b. This is exactly the right-angled triangle of the Pythagoras demo.

The plot at the bottom repeats this for every frequency the slider can take. It has a peak at 2 Hz of height 1.0, and a peak at 5 Hz of height 0.6, which are the amplitudes of the two waves that made the signal. The signal has been analysed into its frequencies.

Between the peaks, the amplitude is not quite zero for a frequency that does not make a whole number of cycles in the second that is looked at. Part of the probe's last cycle is missing, so the blue and orange areas no longer cancel completely. This is called spectral leakage.
