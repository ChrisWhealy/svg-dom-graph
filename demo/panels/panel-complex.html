Complex numbers

A data node can hold complex numbers, in single or double precision, and write each one in whichever form suits the job. The Argand diagrams below draw the complex plane: the real part runs along the horizontal axis and the imaginary part up the vertical one, so every number is a point, and every form is a different way of saying where it is.

Rectangular

The two steps along the axes: a across and b up, written a + bi. On the diagram they are the two legs of a right triangle: the adjacent leg along the real axis, in green, and the opposite leg up or down to the dot, in purple. Adding two numbers adds their parts. In a node, a small picture of the two legs begins each cell: the adjacent leg along the real axis, in green, and the opposite leg up or down to the number, in purple.

Polar

The distance from the origin, the modulus r, and the angle θ from the positive real axis, written r ∠ θ. On the diagram they are the solid line and the arc. The angle is measured anticlockwise, so a number below the real axis has a negative angle. Multiplying two numbers multiplies their moduli and adds their angles. The angle can be written in degrees, the default, or in radians. In a node, a small arrow begins each cell, from the origin to the number, so it shows the general direction, and how long each number is against the longest in the node. It is a sparkline and not a plot, so read the value from the text.

Trigonometric and exponential

r (cos θ + i sin θ) is the polar form with the point spelled out as its two legs. Euler's formula says the same thing as r eiθ, which a node draws with the exponent raised and smaller: an i and then the angle. An exponent is always in radians, so the exponential form is, whatever unit the polar form uses.

Log-polar

The pair (ln r, θ). It is the real and imaginary parts of the complex logarithm ln z = ln r + iθ, so a product, which multiplies the moduli, adds the first number of each pair as well as the second. The log of the modulus of zero is -inf.

Matrix

A complex number a + bi turns and stretches the plane, and the real 2×2 matrix with a on the diagonal and -b and b off it does exactly the same. Multiplying two such matrices is multiplying the two numbers. It is drawn as two rows between brackets in a cell that is two rows high.

Hover over a node to see its type: complex64 has f64 parts, and complex32 has f32 parts. Under the hexadecimal and binary formats a complex number shows the bits of its real part and then of its imaginary part.

One number, seven ways

Drag the slider under the diagram to change the real part a, and the one beside it to change the imaginary part b. Each is exactly as long as the axis it lies along, so its thumb is directly under or beside the point of the axis whose value it stands for. The diagram is drawn again, and each of the seven nodes shows the new number in its own form. A slider has the keyboard too: Tab to it, and use the arrow keys, Home and End.

The roots of unity

The numbers that, raised to the power n, give 1. They are evenly spaced round the unit circle, 360°/n apart, and are the twiddle factors of an n-point Fourier transform. In the polar node every modulus is exactly 1 and the angles go up in steps of 360°/n. The slider sets n.
