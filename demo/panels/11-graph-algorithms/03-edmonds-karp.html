Edmonds–Karp Maximum Flow

A network has a source s and a sink t, and there is a limit to how much traffic any particular edge can carry. The maximum flow is the most that can be sent from the source to the sink. The Edmonds–Karp algorithm discovers this maximum by sending traffic along one path at a time.

Each round starts with a breadth-first search of the residual network, which shows where more can still be sent. An edge with room left gives a forward transition. An edge that already carries flow also gives a reverse transition, which takes flow back. A reverse transition is not a new edge: it belongs to the same edge, and using it lowers that edge's flow.

From each vertex the search looks at its transitions in the order of their edge IDs, and it stops the moment it finds the sink. The path it found is the shortest in number of edges. The most that fits along the whole path is its bottleneck, and every edge of the path changes by that amount at once. When a search cannot reach the sink, the vertices it did reach are one side of a minimum cut, and the capacity that crosses it equals the maximum flow.

Reading the picture

  • Original edge: a solid line with a solid arrowhead, labelled flow/capacity
  • Forward residual transition: dashed, with an open arrowhead, labelled with the room left
  • Reverse residual transition: dashed, amber, with an open diamond, labelled “rev” and the flow that could be taken back
  • The vertex being searched from: a thick outline
  • A mark with a number: the vertex’s place in the queue
  • The augmenting path: a thick line, and its changed edges say before→after
  • An edge that crosses the minimum cut from S to T: a thick line

Using the page

The selectors, the buttons and the range are in the scene, above the graph.

  • Next and Previous move one event. Restart goes back to the start. A button that has nowhere to go is dimmed but can still be pressed, and then says so.
  • Previous augmentation and Next augmentation jump between the steps where flow is added, never to the one you are on. Jump to cut goes to the end.
  • Play steps through the run at the speed you choose and stops at the end. Any other button, or the range, stops it. While it plays, only a found path, an augmentation and the cut are announced to a screen reader.
  • The range goes straight to any step. Its arrow keys move it one step at a time.
  • Residual arcs shows or hides the dashed transitions. Queue marks shows or hides the numbers on the graph, and the queue is always in the text. Arc detail marks the transition being looked at, and lists the ones before it. None of them changes the run.
  • Tab to the graph, which is a single stop, and use the arrow keys to move between the vertices and the edges in that order, and Home and End to the first and the last. This only moves the focus: it never changes the step. Clicking a dashed transition focuses the edge that it belongs to.
  • The second graph is built so that the second path goes back along edge e0, which takes flow off it. Its dashed amber transition is the reverse of e0, and e0 goes from 2 to 1 while the total goes from 2 to 3.
