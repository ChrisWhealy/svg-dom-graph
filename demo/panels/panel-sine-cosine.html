Sine and Cosine

You may have been taught in school that "sine" or "cosine" have something to do with calculating the angles inside a right-angled triangle — or you may simply have been in the room when mathematics was being taught…

However, the heart of the matter is that the sine and cosine are deeply related to how a point rotates around the circumference of a circle. The right-angled triangle is simply a graphical tool for understanding that rotation.

Let's draw a circle with a radius of one whose centre is located at the origin (0,0). Next, we mark a point C on the circumference of the circle.

As the point C rotates anti-clockwise around the circle, it forms the apex of a right-angled triangle, where the height is marked by the point H and the width is marked by the point W. The point W swings left and right along the Y axis, and the point H swings up and down along the Y axis.

This left/right swinging motion is plotted on the graph below the circle, and the up/down swinging motion is plotted on the graph to the right of the circle.

Also notice that a sine wave and a cosine wave have exactly the same shape; the only difference being that when the angle crosses zero degrees. When the sine wave first has a value of zero, the cosine wave has a value of one; then, when the sine wave first reaches a value of one, the cosine wave has a value of zero. As a results of this difference, the sine and cosine waves are said to be 90º out of phase with each other.
